Where your keys are

A key is a secret that lets a program use a service, open a folder or read a backup.

  • Your phoneMakes every key. Readable only while the phone is unlocked. yes
  • Your computerOnly after you approve. Held in memory, not written to disk by sealctl, until the session ends. while approved
  • Our relay serverPasses sealed messages between your computer and phone. It cannot open them. never
  • sealctl (us)We have no copy of your keys or your 24 words. never
  • Your paper cardThe 24 words. Every key can be made again from them. words

How your computer gets a key

You approve on your phone

Your phone shows which computer asks, for which key and for which command. Nothing is sent until you tap Allow, and the key is sealed so only that computer can open it.

Each thing has its own key

Your secrets, each folder and each backup have their own key. Knowing one key does not reveal another, or the 24 words.

A work session

  1. You approveThe key arrives, sealed for this computer.
  2. You workThe key stays in memory. You are not asked again.
  3. The session endsYou lock, stop working, take your phone away, or it is 02:00.
  4. The key is goneThe next use asks your phone again.

What happens if…

…you lose your phone

The words on it are readable only while it is unlocked, and sensitive steps also need your face or fingerprint.

  1. Lock or erase it with Find My or Find My Device.
  2. Restore sealctl on a new phone from your backup or card.
  3. Your computers reconnect to it by themselves.

Details

…your laptop is stolen

Locked or switched off, it holds no key that opens your secrets, folders or backups.

  1. In the app: Machines, the laptop, Lock now.
  2. Tap This machine was lost or stolen, then Unpair.
  3. Change the keys it lists at their providers.

Details

…an AI agent asks for a key

The program it runs gets the key; the agent sees ****, and asking sealctl to show a key is refused.

  1. Its first use in a project asks your phone.

Details

…someone takes your unlocked phone

Pairing a computer or showing the 24 words needs your face or fingerprint; the passcode alone is not enough.

  1. Away from trusted places, showing the words waits two hours. A place trusted just now counts only two hours later.
  2. Every computer is told; sealctl cancel stops it.
  3. Lock or erase the phone with Find My.

Details

…you lose your 24-word card

Your phone still has the words, so nothing stops working.

  1. Write a new card: Settings, Show words.

Details

…the relay or sealctl disappears

With your 24 words, the open-source command line tool opens everything, with no phone, server or account.

  1. sealctl recover opens a store, volume or backup.
  2. sealctl unseal --all --words hands everything back.

Details

What sealctl can't do

  • A program you give a key to can use it, or leak it.
  • Harmful software running as you can use what is unlocked during a session.
  • While a session is open, your system can copy keys from memory to disk, in swap or a hibernation file.
  • The AI-agent guards stop mistakes, not a determined attacker.
  • Someone with your unlocked phone, or its passcode, can approve everyday requests.
  • The relay can delay or drop messages. It cannot read or fake them.
  • Changing your 24 words is not in the app yet.
  • If you lose the card, every copy of the words and your phone, nothing can open your things. There is no reset.

How to check this

  • Open source. The command line tool (MIT) and the phone app (AGPL-3.0) are public. The relay's code is not, but how to talk to it is.
  • Standard cryptography only. Published, widely used methods; nothing home-made. For specialists: the list, what the relay sees and threats and what remains.
  • Public protocol. Every message is documented, with test values that the core, the relay and both apps check against.
  • What the relay keeps is listed in the Privacy notice.
  • What we don't claim. No independent audit and no certifications yet. Our own tests, done with the help of AI, are listed below; they are not an audit.

Check a download

The packages, tarballs, PKGBUILD and SHA256SUMS in a release have a build attestation (Sigstore) tying each to the release workflow and the commit it was built from. Check who made a download with the GitHub CLI:

gh attestation verify <file> --repo sealctl/sealctl

The SHA256SUMS file in each release catches a damaged download. It comes from the same place as the files, so it is not proof of who made them. Put it next to your downloads and run:

shasum -a 256 -c SHA256SUMS --ignore-missing      # macOS
sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS --ignore-missing          # Linux

Or build from source.

Report a problem

Report privately through a GitHub security advisory or {{email.security}}, not a public issue. First answer within three working days; fixes are credited in the release notes.

We welcome good-faith security research and will not pursue or support legal action against you if you follow this policy: test only with your own phone, words and machines, and on the relay only your own pairings and identities; never access, change or keep other people's data; no denial-of-service or load tests on the relay, and no testing of Cloudflare's, Paddle's, Apple's or Google's systems; give us 90 days to fix before you publish, sooner by agreement. We treat such research as authorised and will say so if anyone takes action against you for it.
