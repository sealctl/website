What sealctl keeps safe

Programmers' computers are full of secret keys. A key is a long password that lets a program use a service: a cloud server, a payment system, an AI tool. Whoever has a key can use that service as you, and often spend your money.

Usually these keys sit in plain files on the computer. Any program can read them, and so can the AI helpers that now write code for us. sealctl moves them into a locked box and lets them out only when you say yes on your phone. It keeps three kinds of things locked:

  • the secret keys your programs use, and the keys you use to log in to other computers (SSH keys);
  • folders you choose to keep locked;
  • backups of those folders.

How it works, in five pictures

  1. 1

    The 24 words are a recipe

    When you start, the phone app gives you 24 ordinary words, and you write them by hand on a paper card. Every key sealctl ever uses is made from these words, the way every loaf comes from one recipe. Keep the card somewhere safe. With it you can get everything back, even without your phone.

  2. 2

    One key per thing, made one way only

    Each thing sealctl locks gets a label: a random serial number written on it. To make its key, the phone mixes the recipe with that label. A different label gives a different key, so one key opens one thing and nothing else. The mixing only works one way, like a smoothie: easy to blend, impossible to un-blend. A key never gives away the recipe or any other key.

  1. 3

    Your phone is the doorbell

    Your computer never holds the recipe. When it needs a key, it rings your phone. The phone shows who is asking and why: which program, which project, which key. Nothing is handed over until you tap Allow. Then the phone makes just that key and sends it. Later the same day, back at the same desk with your phone unlocked and nearby, the door can open again without a tap.

  2. 4

    The relay is a postman

    Your phone and your computer talk through our server, the relay. Every message travels in a sealed envelope that only the one it is meant for can open, and every reply carries a signature only your phone can make. The postman sees who writes to whom and when. He can't read the letters, and he can't fake one.

  3. 5

    Your computer only borrows the key

    The computer keeps the key in its memory while you work, and never writes it to its disk. When you stop working for a while, when your phone leaves your trusted place, when you tap Lock, or at 2 at night at the latest, it forgets the key. Next time, it rings again.

What happens if…

…you lose your phone

The recipe sits inside the phone behind its screen lock, so a finder can't use it without your passcode.

  1. Lock or erase the phone from another device with Apple Find My or Google Find My Device.
  2. Put sealctl on a new phone from your iCloud or Google backup, or type the words from your card. Your computers find the new phone by themselves.
  3. Change your words in the app, so the old phone can't open anything new.

Step by step

…your laptop is stolen

If it was locked or switched off, the thief finds only locked boxes. No key that opens your things is stored on its disk, and to get one the laptop would have to ring your phone.

If you were in the middle of work, the keys are in its memory, behind your screen lock, until the session ends. On your phone, open the laptop under Machines and tap Lock now, then This machine was lost or stolen. It unpairs the laptop and lists the keys it used, so you can change them. Your 24 words were never on the laptop, so they stay as they are.

Step by step

…an AI agent asks for a key

An AI agent is a program that writes code and runs commands for you. With sealctl it can use a key without seeing it: the key goes into the one program the agent runs, and wherever it would show up in the output, the agent sees ****.

The first time an agent wants a key in a project, your phone asks you, showing the agent, the project, the command and the key. If the agent asks sealctl to show a key, sealctl refuses, and the guards that sealctl ai install adds block the usual tricks, such as printing a key file.

AI agents in the docs

…someone takes your unlocked phone

A plain tap only answers your own computers, the ones you already paired. Anything sensitive, such as adding a new computer, handing your files back unlocked, or showing your 24 words, needs your face or fingerprint every time. The phone's passcode is not enough, and if someone adds a new face or fingerprint, the app asks for two words from your card.

The most sensitive steps also wait two hours when they are approved away from your trusted places, such as your home or office. During those two hours every one of your computers shows a warning, and you can cancel from any of them with sealctl cancel. Meanwhile, lock or erase the phone with Find My or Find My Device, set up a new phone and change your words.

The two-hour wait

…someone sees your 24-word card

The words are the recipe for every key, so look after the card like the key to your home. If someone may have seen it, change your words in the app. Each computer moves to the new words the next time it talks to your phone, and sealctl migrate locks your folders and backups again with the new keys.

Copies someone made before you changed the words still open with the old words. Changing them protects everything from then on.

Change words

…the relay stops, or sealctl disappears

Your things never depend on us. With your 24 words, the free, open-source sealctl command opens any secret store, folder or backup it locked, with no phone, no relay and no account: sealctl recover <path>. And sealctl unseal --all hands everything back under passwords of your own, also from the words alone.

Locked folders are ordinary encrypted disks (APFS on a Mac, LUKS on Linux), and backups are ordinary restic backups, so common tools still read them. While the relay is down, your phone can't approve new requests; your card still opens everything.

Leaving sealctl

The relay's own code is not public. It holds nothing that opens your things: the way to talk to it is published, every reply it carries is signed by your phone, and your things open without it. What it keeps is listed in the Privacy notice.

What sealctl can't do

  • Stop a program from leaking a key you gave it. When you let a program use a key, that program has it. A bad program, or a bad package hidden in your project, could send it somewhere. sealctl gives each command only the keys named for it, and your phone writes down every use.
  • Beat harmful software that runs as you while you work. While a session is open, such software can use whatever is unlocked, just as you can. Lock when you step away; sealctl also locks by itself when you stop working.
  • Stop a determined attacker with its AI guards. The guards catch the usual ways an agent could see a key, by mistake or out of curiosity. Software that sets out to get around them can.
  • Help once someone is past your screen lock while a session is open, or has your phone and its passcode. Lock the computer from your phone, lock or erase the phone, and change your words.
  • Bring back lost words. If you lose the card, every copy of the words and your phone, nothing can open your things. There is no reset, not even by us.
  • Make the postman deliver. The relay can't open or fake messages, but it could hold them back. Then nothing is approved until they arrive.
  • Show an outside audit. No independent company has reviewed sealctl yet, and we hold no certifications. This changes when that does.

Check what you download

Every release comes with a SHA256SUMS file: a fingerprint for each file. If a file was changed on the way to you, its fingerprint won't match. Download the list next to your files and check them:

shasum -a 256 -c SHA256SUMS --ignore-missing      # macOS
sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS --ignore-missing          # Linux

Or build sealctl yourself from its source code: cargo install --locked --git https://github.com/sealctl/sealctl sealctl. See Install.

Report a problem

Found something that looks like a security problem? Tell us privately, so it can be fixed before anyone misuses it: through a private GitHub security advisory on the affected repository, or by email to {{email.security}}. Please don't open a public issue.

You get a first answer within three working days. Fixed issues are credited in the release notes unless you prefer otherwise. Machine-readable contacts: security.txt. Policy: SECURITY.md.
